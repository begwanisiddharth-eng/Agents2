## In Favor: Psychedelics Should Be Allowed for Treating Psychiatric Diseases

Allowing psychedelics for psychiatric treatment is a **measured, evidence-based public health decision**: there is already credible clinical evidence, strong biological plausibility, and a clear pathway to regulated, safer use.

### 1) The clinical evidence shows meaningful benefits
Multiple high-quality randomized trials indicate that psychedelics can reduce symptoms in otherwise difficult-to-treat conditions:

- **Treatment-resistant depression (TRD):** In large Phase 2/3 studies, **psilocybin** produced **rapid and clinically significant reductions** in depressive symptoms compared with control conditions. Notably, effects can persist beyond the dosing session, unlike many traditional antidepressant onsets that take weeks.
- **Major depressive disorder with cancer (anxiety/depression spectrum):** Trials using psilocybin have reported substantial symptom improvements with measurable durability over follow-up periods.
- **PTSD and substance-use comorbidity:** Emerging controlled studies and meta-analytic reviews suggest psychedelics may reduce PTSD symptom severity and can improve outcomes related to substance use when delivered in structured therapy models.

The key point is not that psychedelics are “cures,” but that **trial data demonstrates therapeutic signal**—enough to justify legal allowance under medical supervision.

### 2) Compared with current options, psychedelics can address a major treatment gap
Psychiatric care faces a major reality: a significant subset of patients does not respond adequately to standard treatments (e.g., multiple antidepressant trials, psychotherapy, ECT/TMS). For TRD especially, the burden is severe—higher disability, relapse, and risk.

Psychedelics offer a potentially **faster-acting** and **response-generating** option. Even conservative estimates from clinical studies show **meaningful proportions** of participants achieve clinically significant improvement. In public health terms, that can translate into fewer years lived with disabling symptoms for patients who currently have limited options.

### 3) Safety is improving with regulation and clinical protocols
The fear argument often assumes psychedelics are uniformly dangerous. But the evidence increasingly supports that **risk is manageable when use is medical and structured**:

- Most clinical trial protocols include **screening** for contraindications (e.g., personal/family psychosis risk), careful dosing, and supervision.
- Adverse events are typically transient (e.g., nausea, anxiety during sessions), and serious harms are not observed at the same frequency as commonly feared—particularly compared to risks from untreated severe depression (suicide, hospitalization, chronic decline).
- The strongest predictor of safety is **not legalization by default**, but **regulated administration**, clinician oversight, and therapy integration.

Legal allowance means **more oversight**, not less. Regulation is the difference between medical use and uncontrolled use.

### 4) “Allowed” does not mean “unrestricted”—it means regulated medical access
The motion supports allowing psychedelics **for treatment of psychiatric diseases**, which can be implemented through:
- licensing requirements for clinics,
- standardized dosing protocols,
- mandatory patient screening and informed consent,
- outcome monitoring and adverse-event reporting,
- limits to specific diagnostic indications and after failure of conventional treatments.

This is how policymakers should approach any powerful therapy: **control quality, reduce harm, and expand access to evidence-based treatment**.

### Conclusion
Psychedelics should be allowed because the evidence base is strong enough to justify **regulated therapeutic use** for psychiatric conditions with substantial unmet need. With careful screening, clinician supervision, and standardized protocols, psychedelics can become a **safe, medically supervised addition** to psychiatric care—improving outcomes for patients who otherwise have few effective options.
